Why we start at the root

Children are enrolled. Most are not learning to read, and many leave early.

82% of children in Uganda at late primary age cannot read and understand a simple text (learning poverty, as defined by the World Bank). Learning poverty and household poverty feed each other: when money runs short, the child is kept home or sent to work, and leaves unable to read.

World Bank, Uganda Learning Poverty Brief, 2024.

So HuSA works on the classroom, the household and the household economy at the same time, through one channel. We build every child for every road they may take after Primary Seven.

Who we work for

Priority without exclusion

HuSA works with refugee and rural host community children in Kyangwali, and the school is open to every family. We give priority to children whose home situation puts the biggest barrier between them and learning, and name each barrier at entry so it has a response. Poverty, displacement and marginalisation are circumstances that can change, not who a child is: as each barrier is removed, the child learns, and the cycle of intergenerational poverty breaks. Children across refugee, post-conflict and rural Africa face the same barriers, so the model is built for community-led and refugee-led schools to run.

Priority groupThe barrierHow the design responds

Children in single-parent households

BarrierOne adult carries income and care alone. When money runs short the child is sent to work or kept home.

ResponseFree early childhood years, fees paid from garden and savings income, weekly household visits.

Children raised by grandparents or elderly caregivers

BarrierCaregivers who cannot walk children to school, pay small costs or help with schoolwork.

ResponseEnrolment as a first protection step, household visits that bring the school to the home, adapted garden roles.

Children with disabilities, and children whose parent has a disability

BarrierBuildings, teaching and assessment that do not fit; reduced household income and services that miss the household.

ResponseWe start by assessing what each child or parent can do, their different abilities, and build on those strengths to remove the barriers: ordinary classes with a floating inclusion teacher and individual plans, accessible parent sessions, and roles in savings and garden work matched to what each person does best.

Unaccompanied, separated and orphaned children

BarrierNo parent to enrol them, follow their learning or keep them safe; caregivers stretched across several children.

ResponsePriority enrolment and close follow-up with the child protection partners responsible for their care.

Overage and out-of-school children

BarrierYears out of class, and shame at sitting with much younger children.

ResponseThe Accelerated Education Pathway, and teaching grouped by level rather than age.

Two-parent households where basic needs go unmet

BarrierThe household cannot keep the child fed, safe and in school.

ResponseThe same household pathway. The barrier is assessed at enrolment, never assumed.

How it works

The school is our way into the household

A settlement has few places that bring the same households together every day. A school does. Household support is tied to the child being in school, so the parent's interest and the child's interest sit on the same side.

Professional teachers teach. Teaching assistants from the community support learning in class and carry out home visits, GBV prevention and early identification of protection cases. Specialist staff take the technical cases. One trusted delivery system serves every thematic area, so we do not pay for separate teams to reach the same families.

Our story

From 137 households to a school

HuSA was founded in 2020 with an emergency food response to 137 households in Kyangwali Refugee Settlement. It came during the COVID-19 lockdown, when casual work stopped and with it the daily income most households ate from. Sumi Hamid, who grew up in the settlement, asked everyone he could reach for help. Think Humanity and Pascal Nzabahandi answered, and together they reached 137 households, most of them headed by single parents or elderly caregivers, identified through the Local Chairpersons.

People kept describing the food as charity from somewhere far away. Sumi told them plainly that he lived in the same settlement and that their positions could easily have been reversed. The organisation grew out of that exchange.

From the start, HuSA belonged to more than one person. In 2022, Sumi took the idea to classmates from his social work and social administration course and to neighbours in the settlement. They met many times and put their own money together to register HuSA as a community-based organisation, which happened in January 2023. Among them were Saufa, Mary, Mwamini Vicky, Akwii Brenda, Esengo Gahinja, the chairperson of Kasonga village, the settlement’s women representative at the time, who later resettled in France, and the parish chief of that time, with many others beside them.

In 2024, Sumi marked his birthday by visiting 10 homes in Zone E, the area where the school now stands. In many homes, children of school age were at home looking after their younger brothers and sisters. Their parent, most often a single parent, had left early to find something to put on the table that day. That is the routine every morning: casual work, digging in someone’s garden, hawking charcoal, firewood or whatever there is to sell. Some homes had no one in them at all. In others, the children went along each day to help.

The pain sits in watching other children go to school. One mother told him: “I feel so bad when I see other children go to school and mine are walking with me. I pass the school gate every morning.” When a home cannot meet a child’s needs, the child moves out to look for them, and once a child is living on the street it becomes hard to bring them home. More and more children in Kyangwali are connected to the streets, and it is hard to trace which homes they came from.

Those visits, and 5 years of following the households first reached in 2020, produced the finding the whole model rests on. Children were enrolling in growing numbers and most were still not learning to read, and we had not found a programme in Zone E working on the household, the classroom and the household economy at the same time. What the child needs and what the household needs have to be identified together and moved forward together, because if one is left behind, the child does not reach the goal. HuSA Model Nursery and Primary School opened in Zone E in 2025, with 59 children and no money set aside, to do that work and to test it before HuSA asks any other school to run it.

Why we start with these children

Today's children become the people who help the next child

Some parents will pass away. Families will continue to separate. Children will continue to grow up with elderly caregivers, and the world will continue to have refugees. The children we work with today carry those stories themselves.

A child who grows up reading, staying in school and earning, in a household that learned to carry them, grows up with conviction. Wherever life takes them, that conviction is what they will use to help the next child carrying the same story.

HuSA itself began this way. Our founder grew up with separated parents and elderly grandparents, came to Uganda as a refugee, and lost both parents within a month of each other. He left school and returned to it years later. Many of our founding members and today’s team carry stories of displacement, separation and interrupted schooling of their own.

We believe the person who lifts another is often the one who has carried the same pain. It is also why we value what a person can do as much as the papers they hold: much of what our team knows was learned working beside others. Our hope for every child is that they step out one day, tell their own story, and lift others facing the barriers they faced. This is how we start small in Zone E and grow across refugee, post-conflict and rural Africa, through the conviction we build in people.

Questions people ask us

Answers to the questions we hear most

Tap a question to see the answer. If yours is not here, write to us and we will reply.

Is HuSA an education organisation or a child protection organisation?

Education is our strategic area and entry point, and protection is built into how we deliver it. Every child we enrol is seen daily at school and weekly at home by our community teaching assistants, who refer any concern the same day. The school is our way into the household, so the child learns, stays in school and stays safe.

Who does HuSA work with?

Refugee children in Kyangwali and children in the rural host communities around it, with the school open to every family. We give priority to children facing the biggest barriers at home: single-parent or grandparent-led homes, children with disabilities or a parent with a disability, unaccompanied, separated and orphaned children, overage and out-of-school children, and homes where basic needs go unmet. Poverty, displacement and marginalisation are circumstances that can change, not who a child is: as each barrier is removed, the child learns, and the cycle of intergenerational poverty breaks.

Is the school only for refugee children?

No. Host community families enrol too, and families who can pay the full fee are welcome. Their fees help fund the places we reserve for priority children.

Why is support tied to the child being in school?

So the parent's interest and the child's interest sit on the same side. Every household knows the support exists because this child is in school, and keeping the child in class becomes the parent's own commitment. Where attendance falls, the household is visited and supported, never cut off.

Do parents pay school fees?

Early childhood is free for 3 years. From Primary One, fees rise in steps and are paid from the household's own garden and savings income, deducted at source, until the household pays in full by Primary Seven.

What happens to the children when a project ends?

The school and the household are still there. The child stays enrolled, our teaching assistants keep visiting the household, and the household keeps moving along its own fee pathway, so the child stays in school and stays seen.

Can other schools use the HuSA model?

Yes. The HCTL model is built for community-led and refugee-led schools, where families pay fees: when a household cannot pay, the child is often kept home or sent to work, and some are drawn towards the streets and exploitation. These schools run the model with their own teachers, starting with Whole Educator, Whole Child teacher development and a free baseline reading assessment; a written agreement follows. Four schools with over 1,800 children are in discussion. In free government and UNHCR-supported schools, we work with the school and partners through the school-based design.

How governance works

Built so that HuSA does not depend on one person

Governance runs on 4 levels, so that mandate, oversight, delivery and community accountability sit with different people: the General Assembly, the Board, management, and the community structures that hold the school to account.

Independent oversight

The Chairperson and the Treasurer are independent and hold no management role. Board members are not paid, and each contributes UGX 100,000 a month to HuSA.

Three Board committees

Finance and Audit, chaired by Akwii Brenda. Programmes and Safeguarding, chaired by Tusingwire Damuru. Human Resource and Nomination, chaired by Baujo Isaac.

Not dependent on the founder

The founder holds no office because he founded HuSA. The Partnerships and Grants Director speaks to donors directly, and Board members meet staff every week or month to solve problems together.

Working with UNHCR and child protection partners

Keeping every child seen, during a project and after it ends

Every child in a protection caseload, even at 2 or 3, will one day be an adult and a parent. Projects and case plans run for a set time, but a child's need for a trusted adult nearby continues. A school and a community-based organisation see the child every day, during a project and long after it.

The child can stay in the government or UNHCR-supported school they attend, or learn at HuSA Model School or a school running the HCTL model, with follow-up agreed with the school's leadership. Community teaching assistants see the child daily, visit the household weekly, and refer any concern the same day through the existing pathway. When a project closes, the school and the household are still there, and the child grows into an adult who reads, earns and raises children who start ahead. We welcome your experience in shaping how this works in Kyangwali.

Helping more cases reach their goals and close

Why it is called transformative

Transformation starts at home, with questions only parents can answer

We do not tell parents why education matters. We ask open-ended questions, in parent sessions and savings groups, and let each parent reach their own conclusion. This follows Jack Mezirow's Transformative Learning Theory: lasting change starts when people, safely and among peers, look again at what they have always believed. It begins with a disorienting dilemma and moves through critical reflection and shared recognition that others carry the same struggle. Each question below carries one step, leading to a perspective transformation each parent reaches for themselves.

  1. Looking back Disorienting dilemma

    Before you depended on support, what opportunities did you have? What were you able to do on your own, and how was your life then?

    Parents remember what they achieved without outside help, and see their own strength again.

  2. Comparing 2 children Critical assessment of assumptions

    Think of a family where one child went to school and another did not. How are their lives and their livelihoods different today?

    Parents see, in a family they know, what schooling changes over a lifetime.

  3. Looking at ourselves Self-examination

    Many of us did not have the chance to go to school. How many ways do we have to earn, compared with someone who is educated?

    Parents discover that the educated person can do everything they can do, plus all that school taught them. Their opportunities are ours, and more.

  4. What we leave behind Exploring new roles

    If you were no longer here, what would you want to leave for your children?

    The conversation moves from today's needs to the child's future.

  5. Who we invest in Perspective transformation

    If not education, what else will change this household? Now that we are older, who else can we invest in to break the poverty that passes from one generation to the next?

    Each parent reaches the answer themselves: the child's education is the household's best investment.

Mezirow describes 3 dimensions of this change, and each one shows in the household: psychological, as parents see their own strength again; convictional, as their belief about education is revised and their conviction grows; and behavioural, as the household works, saves and pays fees. For Mezirow, transformation is complete only when it leads to action.

Mezirow’s 10 phases, and where each shows in our modelShow the phasesHide the phases
  1. Phase 1

    A disorienting dilemma

    A person meets a reality their old assumptions cannot explain: a crisis, a loss, displacement or a hard fact.

    In our model: The honest naming of the household’s reality at Entry on the E4Rs pathway, and our first question to parents.

  2. Phase 2

    Self-examination

    The person looks honestly at their own feelings about the dilemma: fear, guilt, anger or grief.

    In our model: The Heal stage, social and emotional learning, and community-based sociotherapy.

  3. Phase 3

    Critical assessment of assumptions

    The person questions beliefs they had taken for granted, such as “education is not for children like mine”.

    In our model: Our slogan in practice: A Known Problem is not a Problem.

  4. Phase 4

    Recognition that the struggle is shared

    The person discovers that others have faced the same dilemma and moved through it.

    In our model: Circle Coach household visits, PTC+ sessions and savings groups.

  5. Phase 5

    Exploring new roles

    With old assumptions loosened, the person imagines new possibilities: the caregiver as co-teacher, the household as an economic unit.

    In our model: Project-based learning and the school garden programme.

  6. Phase 6

    Planning a course of action

    The person sets a deliberate plan.

    In our model: Each household co-designs its own plan along the E4Rs pathway.

  7. Phase 7

    Acquiring knowledge and skills

    The plan needs competence, not only intention.

    In our model: Teaching at the Right Level, the Accelerated Education Pathway, savings group training and garden skills. Our school motto: Knowledge is the foundation of action.

  8. Phase 8

    Trying new roles

    The person tests the new role where the risk is low.

    In our model: A first garden cycle, a first small loan, a parent leading part of a session.

  9. Phase 9

    Building competence and confidence

    Repeated success turns the new role into the person’s own.

    In our model: The Transform stage, and the Production and Investment drivers of the Flywheel.

  10. Phase 10

    Reintegration

    The person lives on the new perspective and begins to lead others.

    In our model: The Lead stage: graduates become coaches, savings group leaders and advocates.

  11. The outcome

    Perspective transformation

    A more open frame of reference that the person acts on. For Mezirow, transformation is incomplete until it produces action.

    In our model: Each parent reaches their own conclusion about the child’s education, then the household acts on it.

Transformation for the child

No child leaves school with nothing in hand

A child who cannot continue after Primary Seven still needs something they can use. Our design prepares every child for all 3 roads after Primary Seven, so whichever road a child takes, they leave able to read, write, do maths and earn.

That is the second meaning of transformative in our model: what we do in school for the child, and what we do at home with the parents, aim at the same result.

Research names 3 kinds of education: transmission, from teacher to pupil; transactional, through experience, inquiry and working with others; and transformation, a change in how a person sees themselves. Our classrooms use the second, through project work and teaching at the right level, so each child reaches the third: seeing themselves as able to read, earn and choose their road.

The root cause

Household poverty and learning poverty feed each other

Our analysis, from 6 years of work in Kyangwali, is that household poverty is the biggest root cause of children leaving school unable to read. It runs in a cycle.

When household poverty deepens, a child is taken out of class to work, care for siblings or save the cost of school. The child leaves without confident reading, writing and maths, without the habit of thinking a problem through, and without a usable skill; some can read, yet still have neither. That child grows into an adult who struggles to earn, and raises the next child in the same poverty.

The cycle turns where the child's schooling and the household's economy meet, so we work on both together, starting with the child, years before a young person would reach a youth programme. We call it transforming Africa from the roots.

Poverty does not explain everything. The 18 children HuSA taught from Primary One moved from three-letter words to reading stories while living in the same households. How a child is taught also decides whether the child learns.

The loop we build

Where the school and the home meet, a new loop turns

In systems thinking, the school and the home are the leverage point, where a change turns the whole loop. There, support tied to the child in school keeps the child learning, the parent's conviction grows, and the household earns, saves and pays fees, so the child stays.

How the model runs

One way in, one delivery system

Support tied to the child in school

Household support is tied to the child attending school, and every household hears why: the support exists because this child is in school. That link, and what parents see their children learning, makes keeping the child in school the parent's own commitment. Where attendance falls, the household is visited and supported, never cut off.

Teaching assistants from the community

They support learning in class and serve as the frontline protection presence in homes: household visits, GBV prevention and early identification of cases. They are trusted because they are from the community and speak the household's language. Specialist protection staff take the technical cases.

Leaders already connected to the school

Livelihoods and environment work runs through the same school and household channel. Church leaders who are also parents of enrolled children carry the message into the villages. Every household is reachable through the same people, at a fraction of the cost of separate outreach teams.

The learner journey

From Baby Class to Primary Seven

Our pedagogy. We teach from where each child is. Teachers build on what the child knows and has lived, group children by level rather than age, and set clear goals in reading and maths. Learning is play-based in early childhood, project-based from Primary One and competency-based in the skill pathway. It is trauma-informed throughout, with a daily Healing Circle before lessons, because a child who feels safe can learn. Teachers check each child's reading to decide what to teach next.

Baby, Middle and Top Class

Early childhood

Three years, free. Play-based early literacy and number sense, a daily Healing Circle, routines, meals and protection observation.

Learning goal: school readiness. Top Class graduates to Primary One.

Primary One to Three

Foundation

Daily Teaching at the Right Level in reading and maths, writing every day, project work from Primary One.

Learning goal: the child reads, writes and does number work at their level.

Primary Four to Six

Skill pathway

Each learner chooses a skill in Primary Four, by interest and by what the local market pays for, and trains in it alongside project-based learning.

Learning goal: a skill the learner can sell or be employed for, shown in a portfolio of work and a competency record. Where a recognised assessment such as UVTAB is open to the learner, a certificate is added value.

Primary Seven

Leaving year

Kept clear for the Primary Leaving Examination, with the skill kept in practice and a transition plan made with the caregiver.

Learning goal: ready for whichever road comes next, with a graduation certificate and competency record; the PLE result follows.

The skill pathway is designed, not yet operating; the list of skills and the year the first cohort reaches Primary Four are being confirmed. Where a recognised assessment such as UVTAB is open to a learner, we help them take it, but the skill they leave with does not depend on it.

After Primary Seven

Three roads, and every child built for all of them

We cannot predict which child will continue, so we build for all 3 roads in every child from Baby Class.

Road 1

Secondary school

Uganda's lower secondary curriculum has been competency-based since 2020 (NCDC). A HuSA graduate who has learned this way for 7 years, and already holds a marketable skill, arrives ahead.

Road 2

Technical school

The graduate continues the chosen skill at a higher level, starting from what they can already do, and can have it certified on the national framework as they progress (UVTAB certifies Levels 1 to 4). Roads 1 and 2 are equal; both can lead to a diploma or a degree.

Road 3

Leaves education

When the household can no longer carry the cost, the graduate still earns from a marketable, employable skill, within the law for their age, and can return to learning later. Road 3 is never failure.

Our protection rule

"Able to earn" never means a child taken out of class or sent to work. The skill is learned in school. Most Primary Seven graduates are 12 to 14, so earning means light, supervised work that does not harm health or schooling, never hazardous work (Employment Act 2006). HuSA follows every graduate for 2 years and keeps the door back to education open.

The first reading result

Eighteen children, and the figure we are watching

In 2026 HuSA assessed all 96 learners in Primary One to Four. The 18 who started Primary One at HuSA in 2025 could read three-letter words when promoted and now read stories (HuSA teacher assessment, 2026).

The other 78 joined in higher classes from elsewhere, and their levels at entry are what the TaRL groups are built around. Eighteen children are one small cohort, so we report this as a first signal, not a proven result. The figure to watch is how quickly the 78 close the distance.

An Early Grade Reading Assessment study across HuSA and 2 neighbouring schools, with independent enumerators, will set the baseline.

Our strategy 2026 to 2031

One way into the household, and a plan to 2031

Our strategy starts from the facts we face, names the one thing we can be the best at, and shows how each step turns the next.

The Stockdale Paradox

We face the hardest facts, and we keep the highest aspiration

The hard facts

Most children in refugee, post-conflict and rural African communities reach age 10 unable to read and understand a simple text (learning poverty, as defined by the World Bank). Household poverty is its biggest root cause, and the two feed each other.

  • 82% of Ugandan children at late primary age cannot read with understanding (World Bank, 2024)
  • Kyangwali's primary schools hold over 47,000 learners, yet only 596 are in Senior One (NRC, 2026)
  • Many parents did not have the chance to go to school. Fees compete with food. Funding usually comes in project cycles, each for one sector, so joining the parts takes deliberate design.
and
Our highest aspiration

Every child in refugee, post-conflict and rural African communities reads, stays in school and leaves able to earn, in a household that can carry them without HuSA.

By 2031: 500+ learners, 200+ self-funding households, 70% of school costs paid by the community, and community-led and refugee-led schools running the model.

The Hedgehog Concept

The one thing we aim to do better than anyone

What we do best

Using the school as the way into the household, so every child learns to read and every household can pay for it. TaRL, savings groups and protection exist elsewhere; HuSA runs them as one system, through one staff team and one channel into every household.

Deeply passionate about

Children in refugee, post-conflict and rural African communities facing the biggest barriers to learning: single-parent and grandparent-headed households, orphaned and separated children, overage and out-of-school children, and children or parents with disabilities. Our founder and team carry lived experience of displacement.

What drives our economic engine

Households move from receiving support to paying fees, from their own garden and savings income, deducted at source. Full-fee families and enterprise partnerships add the rest. Whoever comes to HuSA leaves as a partner.

Our hedgehog statementIf the school becomes the way into the household, and support is tied to the child in school, then every child in refugee, post-conflict and rural African communities reads, stays and leaves able to earn, in a household that can pay for it without HuSA.

The Flywheel

Each turn makes the next one easier

Six drivers push the same wheel. Every step builds the momentum for the next, until graduates lead and fund the next cohort.

  1. Protection: support is tied to the child in school. Conviction keeps the child in school.
  2. Wellbeing: Healing Circles and social emotional learning. Regulated children are ready to learn.
  3. Foundational learning: TaRL, AEP and project-based learning. Every child reads, writes and does maths.
  4. Livelihoods: SGALP school and household gardens. Gardens feed and earn.
  5. Ownership: VSLA, SAVER and fees. Households pay fees from their own income.
  6. Leadership: Circle Coaches and PTC+. Graduates lead and fund the next cohort.

Sustainability

How the work lasts, with its status

1. A school built to attract

Committed

A well-built, fenced and furnished school draws families who can pay the full fee. HuSA plans 60% of primary places for them and reserves 40% for priority children, whose places full-fee families help fund. So classroom construction is a sustainability investment. The 60:40 split and full fee are in the 2027 budget, and become achieved once permanent classrooms are built and the first full-fee intake enrols.

2. Leaders become partners

Committed

Through the Entrepreneurial Leadership pathway, every HuSA leader is prepared to own a business, with HuSA holding shares in each one, and the returns fund HuSA's programmes. HuSA puts no capital into the businesses. Every return is applied to HuSA's objects, and none is paid to members, Board members or staff. Cohort 1 is designed and budgeted for 2027.

3. The E4Rs household pathway

In use

Free early childhood, stepped fees and fee collection are running. 23% of households paid consistently before deduction at source, with targets of 58% by the end of Year 1 and 70% by the end of Year 2.

Reach and adoption

Reported in 3 levels, never added together

HuSA runs one school to hold a model that community-led and refugee-led schools across Africa can run. In free government and UNHCR-supported schools, the work reaches priority children through partners, under the school-based design. Adoption starts with professional development, not a signature: Whole Educator, Whole Child training, then the full HCTL model, 2 teachers per school certified as facilitators, and a free baseline reading assessment.

Making that package adaptable and reusable is the purpose of Sumi Hamid's African Leadership Academy Aspire Leadership for Education Fellowship. We do not quote a cost per child for adoption until one training cycle has been delivered and every shilling counted.

Start at the Root

Why generational change in Africa begins with the child in school, and how one school-centred design can carry education, protection and livelihoods together.

By Sumi Hamid, Founder and Executive Director. Published by HuSA Foundation for Systemic Transformation. DOI 10.5281/zenodo.24246709.

Read the paper (PDF) →The HCTL Model

What the paper argues

In 2026 the primary schools of Kyangwali Refugee Settlement held 46,746 children, and 596 students were in Senior One (NRC, Kyangwali Refugee Settlement Schools Profile 2026). Most of a generation leaves school before any youth programme can reach them. The paper argues that generational change has to start earlier: with the child, and inside the household that decides whether that child stays in school.

Reading, writing and maths come first, because they decide who takes part in Africa’s future. The household is where schooling is won or lost. And the school can be the one channel through which education, child protection, GBV prevention, livelihoods and environment work reach the same family.

The HuSA design in two parts

The Household-Centred Transformative Learning (HCTL) Model is the model a school runs, designed for community-led and refugee-led schools where families pay fees.

The School-Based Design is run by HuSA with partners in the free government and UNHCR-supported schools where most children learn, so support continues after a project ends.

Both were designed by Sumi Hamid for HuSA Foundation for Systemic Transformation, which owns them. Schools and partners adopt them by agreement with HuSA.
